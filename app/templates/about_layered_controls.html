🛡️ Understanding Layered Security Controls

Why multiple control failures are less common than you think

The Defense-in-Depth Principle

Effective cybersecurity relies on multiple independent layers of defense. For a sophisticated attack to fully succeed, an attacker often must bypass several controls in sequence. This is much harder than defeating a single control.

If ANY control succeeds, the attack is disrupted or contained

The Mathematics of Independent Failures

When controls operate independently, the probability of all of them failing is calculated by multiplying their individual failure rates. This is called an AND gate in probability theory.

📊 Example: Two Independent Controls

Scenario: An attacker must bypass both email filtering AND endpoint detection

  • A poor quality email filter could have a 30% bypass rate (30% vulnerability)
  • A signature based anti-virus could have a 30% bypass rate (30% vulnerability)
Combined Vulnerability = 0.30 × 0.30 = 0.09 (9%)

Even though each control is only 70% effective, together they reduce vulnerability by 70% (from 30% to 9%)!

📊 Example: Three Independent Controls

Scenario: Perimeter + Email + Endpoint protection

  • Each control: 60% vulnerability (not very strong individually)
Combined Vulnerability = 0.60 × 0.60 × 0.60 = 0.216 (22%)

Three mediocre controls reduce vulnerability by 64% compared to one!

Why This Matters for Your Assessment

When you select a control level (e.g., "Basic antivirus protection"), our simplified model assigns a single vulnerability number. However, if you have additional layered controls in place, the actual combined vulnerability is lower.

The 25% reduction toggle provides a simplified adjustment to account for this layered defense effect without requiring you to model each control individually.

⚠️ Limitations of the Simplified Model

The 25% blanket reduction is a rough estimate. In reality:

  • Different controls have different effectiveness rates
  • Some controls are partially dependent (not fully independent)
  • Attack paths may bypass certain control combinations
  • Control effectiveness varies by threat type and attacker capability
  • Failure correlation matters (common mode failures)

For critical decisions, a more precise analysis is recommended.
